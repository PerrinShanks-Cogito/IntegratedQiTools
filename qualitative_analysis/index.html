Report what you found and why it matters, not how many people said it. A topic raised by a few people can matter a lot: cold food is likely to affect everyone on the ward, not just the two who mentioned it, and experiences of small groups, such as people with a learning disability, will never show up in large numbers. Judge each finding by its impact.

Method and limitations

Be clear about how you analysed the comments and what that means for your findings. Leave this blank to use the suggested wording shown.

Qualitative data is words and images. It helps you understand why and how, where numbers tell you how many, how much or how often. Best practice is to read all of it, then choose a method that fits. Sentiment and content analysis sort comments by the words people used, into categories or topics. They suit short, focused (“thin”) comments and can be done by non-experts, but they can oversimplify. Thematic analysis looks at tone, inference and context to find themes. It suits long, detailed (“thick”) comments and is best done by, or with, an experienced qualitative analyst.

Using the results: word clouds are for getting to know your data, not for reporting. Don’t make decisions from counts of coded comments: a finding’s significance comes from its impact, not how often it was mentioned. Present findings in words, with a short description and quotes that show where each finding came from, and say what method, sample and limitations your analysis had. Check comments for personal details and safeguarding concerns before you analyse or share them, and follow your organisation’s information governance and research ethics rules.
