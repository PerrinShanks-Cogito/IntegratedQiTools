Map, analyse and redesign a process

Swimlane map of what really happens, with value, waits and hand-offs counted for you.

Agree where the process starts and ends before you map, so everyone maps the same thing.

A process map shows what really happens, one step at a time, so the whole team can see the journey from the patient's side, spot the waste and redesign around it. This builder follows the NHS Improvement Leaders' Guide on process mapping, analysis and redesign: map the current process, analyse it, then design and test a better one.

Start or end: the agreed boundaries of the process. Activity: something someone does. Start it with a verb. Decision: a question with a yes and a no route. Link the no route if it sends work back. Wait or queue: time where nothing happens to the patient or the work. Value-adding: changes things for the patient in a way they would want. Necessary, non-value-adding: needed for safety, law or the system, but the patient wouldn't miss it. Non-value-adding: waste. Waiting, chasing, repeating, re-keying. H, hand-off: the work or the patient passes to another person or team. Rework loop: a "no" that sends work back to an earlier step.

Before you map

  1. Pick a process that matters to patients and staff, and that you have some ability to change.
  2. Agree the start and end points and how much detail you need. A high-level map first, then detail where the problems are.
  3. Bring together people who work in every part of the process, and patients or carers where you can. No single person knows the whole journey.
  4. Find a facilitator who is not too close to the process to ask naive questions.

Map the current process

  • Map what actually happens most of the time, not what the policy says should happen. Walk the process if you can.
  • One step per box, worded as an action. Give each role or team its own lane so you can see every time the patient is passed on.
  • Map supporting processes, such as samples, notes or requests, in their own lanes alongside the patient's journey rather than mixed into it.
  • Capture frustrations and ideas as issue notes as you go. They become your list of things to fix.
  • Expect it to be messier than anyone thought. That is the point.

Analyse it

  • Count the steps, the steps the patient goes through, the hand-offs and the steps that add no value. The Analyse tab does this for you.
  • Look for delays and queues, bottlenecks, duplication, loops where work is sent back, and steps that only exist to check or chase.
  • Ask of every step: would the patient value this? Classify it as value-adding, necessary or waste.
  • Focus on the process, not the people in it.

Redesign

  • Remove steps that add no value, then simplify the ones that are necessary.
  • Cut hand-offs: can one person do more of the journey?
  • Do things in parallel rather than one after another, and move steps so they happen once, at the right time.
  • Find the bottleneck and protect it. Time lost there is lost for the whole process.
  • Design for the common route and handle the exceptions separately.

Test the changes

Use the Model for Improvement: what are we trying to accomplish, how will we know a change is an improvement, and what change can we make? Test the redesign in small PDSA cycles rather than switching it on everywhere at once, and track measures such as lead time, number of steps and hand-offs over time on a run chart.

Terms

  • Lead time: total time from the start to the end of the process, waits included.
  • Value-adding time: the part of the lead time spent on value-adding steps.
  • Bottleneck: the step with the least capacity, where work queues.
  • Hand-off: a point where the patient or the work moves from one person or team to another.

Source: NHS Institute for Innovation and Improvement, Improvement Leaders' Guide 1.2, Process mapping, analysis and redesign (NHS England Improvement Hub). Value colours follow the QSIR value stream mapping convention.
