Design and analyse an experiment

Plan a two-level factorial experiment with two, three or four factors, record the result of each run, and see which factors, and which combinations of them, really change the result. Everything runs in your browser, so your data is never uploaded.

Your experiment

Each replicate runs every combination of settings once more. Repeats let the tool tell real effects from run-to-run noise.

For each factor, give the two settings you will compare. Choose Numeric for a quantity, such as the number of nurses, and Text for a choice, such as old or new letter.

Model

Untick terms to take them out of the model. Taking out a factor also takes out the interactions that include it, and putting an interaction back puts its factors back, so the model always stays valid.

Settings

Runs and results

Carry out the runs in run order, which is shuffled so that anything else changing over time doesn’t get mistaken for an effect. Type or paste each result into the last column; pasting a column from Excel fills the rows below too.

Result

What it does: a two-level factorial experiment changes several factors at once, trying every combination of a low and a high setting for each one, so 3 factors give 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 combinations. Testing them together uses fewer runs than changing one thing at a time, and it shows interactions: when the effect of one factor depends on the setting of another. The tool fits a model to the results and tests each factor (main effect) and each interaction.

Reading the results: the effect is how much the result changes, on average, when a factor moves from its low to its high setting. The coded coefficient is half the effect, measured from the overall average, with every factor coded −1 for low and +1 for high. The uncoded coefficients give the prediction equation in the real units of numeric factors; text factors stay coded −1 and +1. A term is a clear effect when its p-value is below the significance level. With one replicate there is nothing left over to measure noise, so take out the terms least likely to matter (usually the highest-order interactions) to get p-values.

Things to check: run the experiment in the shuffled run order, keep everything else as steady as you can, and record anything unusual. Choose settings far enough apart to make a difference you could see, but still safe and practical. A factorial experiment tests the two settings you chose; the prediction between them assumes a straight line, and beyond them it is a guess. Confirm what you find with a run or a PDSA cycle at the best settings, and track the result over time with a run chart or SPC chart.
