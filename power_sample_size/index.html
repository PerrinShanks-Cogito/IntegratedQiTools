Power and sample size

What it does: before you run a hypothesis test, such as a t test, ANOVA or a test of proportions, it helps to know whether you have enough data to find what you are looking for. Power is the chance that the test will show a significant result when the change you care about really has happened. Give any two of sample size, power and the difference you want to detect, and this works out the third.

Choosing the values: the difference to detect should be the smallest change that would matter in practice, not the change you hope for. Take the standard deviation from your baseline data, for example from a run chart or SPC chart of the measure; a larger standard deviation needs a larger sample. For proportions, the baseline is your current rate and the comparison is the rate you would want to be able to detect. The significance level (α) is the risk of a false alarm you will accept, usually 0.05. Use a one-sided test only if you decided in advance that you only care about a change in one direction.

How it is worked out: power for t tests uses a normal approximation to the noncentral t distribution, and power for one-way ANOVA uses the noncentral F distribution, where the difference is between the largest and smallest group means with the other means midway between them. Tests of proportions use the normal approximation, and their sample sizes come from the usual formula. Sample sizes for 2-sample tests, ANOVA and the 2-proportions test are for each group; for a paired t test, the sample size is the number of pairs.
