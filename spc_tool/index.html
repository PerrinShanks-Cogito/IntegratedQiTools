SPC chart

Paste your data to get a statistical process control chart with process limits, special cause signals and a plain-English reading of what the chart shows, following NHS England's Making Data Count approach.

Other charts are available. As well as the XmR chart, you can make P, U, C and T charts by changing the answer to in the panel on the left.

Use Data sheet to download a spreadsheet, set up for the chart you've chosen, with guidance on collecting your data.

Your XmR chart

How the XmR chart works

Each point is the value for that period, such as the average days from referral to first appointment in a month. The moving range is the difference between each point and the one before it, and shows how much the process varies from one period to the next.

The process limits are the mean ± 2.66 × the average moving range. The moving range chart has an upper limit of 3.267 × the average moving range. If a few very large moving ranges are inflating the limits, you can leave them out when calculating the average.

A special cause is flagged for any of: a point outside the limits; 7 points in a row above or below the mean; 7 points in a row going up or down; or two out of three points in a row close to the same limit (beyond 2 standard deviations, all on the same side of the mean). Blue shows improvement, orange shows concern, and purple shows special cause where neither direction is better. Only recalculate the limits where there is a known change to the process and the data show a sustained shift afterwards. Based on NHS England's Making Data Count guidance.

How the P chart works

Each point is the count divided by its total for that period, such as 412 patients seen within four hours out of 530 who attended (77.7%). The mean is the sum of all the counts divided by the sum of all the totals, so busy periods count for more than quiet ones.

The process limits are the mean ± 3 standard deviations, where the standard deviation for each point is √(mean × (1 − mean) ÷ its total). A smaller total gives wider limits, which is why the limits step in and out. The limits appear once there are 13 points; until then, only runs and trends are checked.

When totals are very large, the limits can get so narrow that almost every point falls outside them. The P′ (Laney) chart widens the limits to allow for the variation between periods: it converts each point to a z-score, takes the average moving range of the z-scores divided by 1.128, and multiplies the standard deviation by that figure.

A special cause is flagged for any of: a point outside the limits; 7 points in a row above or below the mean; 7 points in a row going up or down; or two out of three points in a row close to the same limit (beyond 2 standard deviations, but inside the limit). Blue shows improvement, orange shows concern, and purple shows special cause where neither direction is better. Only recalculate the limits where there is a known change to the process and the data show a sustained shift afterwards. Based on NHS Improvement's SPC P chart tool (version 3.0).

How the U chart works

Each point is the count divided by the exposure for that period, such as 12 falls in 3,850 bed days, shown as a rate: 3.1 falls per 1,000 bed days. The exposure is whatever the events happen in, such as bed days, admissions or operating hours. The mean is the sum of all the counts divided by the sum of all the exposures, so busy periods count for more than quiet ones.

The process limits are the mean ± 3 standard deviations, where the standard deviation for each point is √(mean ÷ its exposure). A smaller exposure gives wider limits, which is why the limits step in and out. A lower limit below zero isn't drawn. The limits appear once there are 13 points; until then, only runs and trends are checked.

When exposures are very large, the limits can get so narrow that almost every point falls outside them. The U′ (Laney) chart widens the limits in the same way as the P′ chart: by the average moving range of the points' z-scores, divided by 1.128.

A special cause is flagged for any of: a point outside the limits; 7 points in a row above or below the mean; 7 points in a row going up or down; or two out of three points in a row close to the same limit (beyond 2 standard deviations, but inside the limit). Blue shows improvement, orange shows concern, and purple shows special cause where neither direction is better. Only recalculate the limits where there is a known change to the process and the data show a sustained shift afterwards.

How the C chart works

Each point is the number of events in that period, such as complaints in a month. A C chart assumes there's about the same opportunity for events each period. If that changes a lot, such as a ward that opens more beds, use a U chart and divide by the exposure instead.

The process limits are the mean ± 3 × √mean, because counts of independent events vary by about the square root of their mean. A lower limit below zero isn't drawn. The limits appear once there are 13 points; until then, only runs and trends are checked.

A special cause is flagged for any of: a point outside the limits; 7 points in a row above or below the mean; 7 points in a row going up or down; or two out of three points in a row close to the same limit (beyond 2 standard deviations, but inside the limit). Blue shows improvement, orange shows concern, and purple shows special cause where neither direction is better. Only recalculate the limits where there is a known change to the process and the data show a sustained shift afterwards.

How the T chart works

Each point is the number of days since the previous event, plotted at the date of the event. The first event starts the clock, so it has no point. Two events on the same day count as half a day apart.

Times between events are skewed: most gaps are short and a few are very long. So the tool converts each gap to its power of 1/3.6 (about 0.2777), which makes the values close to normally distributed. It works out the mean and the average moving range of the converted values, sets the limits at the mean ± 2.66 × the average moving range, and converts the mean and limits back to days with a power of 3.6. When the converted lower limit falls below zero there is no lower limit.

A point above the upper limit is an unusually long gap, so events have become less frequent. A point below the lower limit, or a run of points below the mean, means events are closer together than expected. Points that are inside the limits are worth a look too if they form an unusual pattern, or if far more or fewer than two thirds of them sit in the middle third between the limits.

Only recalculate the limits where there is a known change to the process and the data show a sustained shift afterwards. Based on NHS England's T chart rare events SPC tool (version 2.0).
