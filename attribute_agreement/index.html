Check how well people agree

Paste the ratings that several people (appraisers) gave the same set of cases, such as pressure ulcer categories or triage levels, and see how consistently each person rates, how well they agree with each other and with an expert standard. Use Data sheet to download a spreadsheet to record the study in. Everything runs in your browser, so your data is never uploaded.

Your data

One row for each case (sample). Columns, in this order: a case name or number (optional), the known standard (optional), then each appraiser’s ratings, with their trials side by side, such as Nurse A trial 1, Nurse A trial 2, Nurse B trial 1, Nurse B trial 2.

Ask each person to rate every case more than once, without seeing their earlier answers, to check whether they are consistent with themselves.

Categories

Put the categories in order, lowest first.

    What it does: attribute agreement analysis checks a measurement made by people’s judgement, such as a category, a grade or a pass or fail. It answers four questions. Within appraisers: does each person give the same answer when they see the same case again? Each appraiser versus standard: does each person get the right answer, every time? Between appraisers: does everyone give the same answer? All appraisers versus standard: does everyone get the right answer, every time? If people can’t agree on what they see, changes in your data may come from who did the rating rather than from your improvement.

    Reading the results: the percent matched is the share of cases where every rating agreed, with a 95% confidence interval. Kappa (Fleiss’ kappa) measures agreement beyond what chance would give: 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than chance. When the categories are in order, Kendall’s coefficients are used instead, giving credit for near misses: rating a category 3 ulcer as 2 is closer than rating it as 1. As a guide, above 0.9 is excellent, 0.7 to 0.9 is generally acceptable, below 0.7 needs improvement and below 0 means people disagree more than chance would explain.

    Running a study: use cases that cover the whole range you see in practice, including borderline ones, and aim for at least 20 to 30. Have an expert agree the correct answer for each case. Show the cases in a different random order each time, and leave time between trials so people don’t remember their answers. Use the cases where people disagree to improve definitions, training or the form itself, then check again.
