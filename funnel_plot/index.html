Build a funnel plot

Compare a rate across populations of different sizes, such as falls on each ward or referrals from each GP practice, and see which stand out once size is taken into account. Everything runs in your browser, so your data is never uploaded.

Your data

Three columns, with one row for each population: its name, the number of events or observations (falls, referrals, patients seen), and the population size (bed days, registered patients, admissions). Every row should cover the same time period.

Chart settings

The overall rate is the usual choice. The average of the rates, used in the NHS Improvement spreadsheet, gives small populations as much weight as large ones.

Result

Paste data on the left to build your chart.

How to read it: a funnel plot is a scatter plot of rates against population size. It compares populations over the same time period, for example the rate of referral for a condition from each GP practice. The population size runs along the bottom and the rate up the side. The solid line is the average and the dashed lines are the limits: they are wide for small populations, where chance alone moves the rate a long way, and narrow for large ones, which gives the plot its funnel shape.

What stands out: a point outside the 99.7% limits is unusual: it is very unlikely to be there by chance alone, so investigate further. Start with the data (is everything counted the same way in each place?), then look at differences in the patients or the process. Being outside the limits depends on both the rate and the population size, so the outliers may not be the populations with the highest or lowest rates. A small population can have the highest rate and still be within its limits.

How the limits are worked out: the limits are the average plus or minus 3 standard errors (99.7%), and 1.96 standard errors for the 95% limits, using the binomial standard error for proportions. If any population has more events than its size (for example several falls per patient), the Poisson standard error is used instead. When many points fall outside the limits, real differences between populations, such as case mix, may be making the limits too narrow; treat them as a guide and consider adjusting for case mix.
