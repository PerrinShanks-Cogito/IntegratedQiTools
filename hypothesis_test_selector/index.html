      What it does: a hypothesis test tells you whether a difference or relationship in your data is likely to be real or could easily have happened by chance. Which test to use depends on your data. Think of your result, the output or Y, as depending on one or more inputs, the Xs: Y = f(X). This tool asks what type of data each is, how many inputs (factors) and groups (levels) you have, and whether your data is normally distributed, then points you to a test.

      Types of data: attribute (or discrete) data is counts or categories, such as admitted or not, or which ward a patient was on. Continuous (or variable) data is a measurement on a scale, such as length of stay, waiting time or blood pressure. A factor is an input that can influence the output, such as ward or day of the week, and its levels are the different values it takes, such as Ward A and Ward B. For example, new contacts made by three teams is attribute in and attribute out; sales made by three teams is attribute in and continuous out; sales against length of service is continuous in and out; and new contacts against length of service is continuous in and attribute out.

      Deciding before you look: write down your null hypothesis (usually that there is no difference or no relationship) and what you will do if it is or isn't rejected before you collect the data. Then if the p-value is below your significance level, usually 0.05, reject the null hypothesis: if p is low, the null must go.

      Errors and power: a type I (alpha) error is a false positive, rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true, such as concluding a change worked when it didn't. A type II (beta) error is a false negative, keeping the null hypothesis when it is false, such as missing a change that really did work. Type II errors are more likely with small samples. Power is the chance of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis; use the power and sample size tool to plan how much data you need.

      Checking the assumptions: most of these tests work best when the data is normally distributed. The Anderson-Darling test checks this: its null hypothesis is that the data is normal, so a p-value above 0.05 means you can treat it as normal. The histogram tool runs it for you. Many tests also assume the groups have similar spread: Levene's test is robust when data isn't normal, Bartlett's test is more sensitive to departures from normality, and the F test compares the variances of two groups. Tests also assume the observations are independent of each other; a chi-squared test checks whether two attributes are independent, and a correlation coefficient (Pearson's, or Spearman's for data that isn't normal) whether two measurements are related.

      A guide, not a rule: the road map covers the most common tests. Your data may need something it doesn't show, such as more than two factors, repeated measurements over time or very small counts. If in doubt, talk to an analyst or statistician before you draw conclusions.
